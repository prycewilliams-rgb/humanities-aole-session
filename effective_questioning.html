Section 1: Why Does Questioning Matter? Adran 1: Pam Mae Cwestiynu'n Bwysig?

🇬🇧 English

Questioning is one of the most fundamental tools in any teacher's repertoire. Whether you are teaching about the causes of the First World War, the impact of climate change on Welsh upland communities, or Buddhist teachings on suffering — the quality of your questions shapes the quality of your learners' thinking.

The Curriculum for Wales places enquiry at the heart of the Humanities AoLE. The 'What Matters' statements explicitly call for learners to participate in different methods of enquiry, evaluate evidence, and develop critical, analytical and evaluative skills.

Mae cwestiynu yn un o'r offer mwyaf sylfaenol ym mhecyn cymorth unrhyw athro. P'un a ydych chi'n addysgu am achosion y Rhyfel Byd Cyntaf, effaith newid yn yr hinsawdd ar gymunedau ucheldiroedd Cymru, neu ddysgeidiaeth Fwdhaidd ar ddioddefaint — mae ansawdd eich cwestiynau yn llunio ansawdd meddwl eich dysgwyr.

Section 2: Types of Questions & Bloom's Taxonomy Adran 2: Mathau o Gwestiynau a Thacsonomeg Bloom

🇬🇧 English — The Key Distinction

Lower-order questions require pupils to remember. They tend to be closed, with a single right answer, starting with what, who, when, where.

Higher-order questions require pupils to think. They are open — with a range of possible responses — starting with how, why, which, to what extent.

Cotton (1988): a combination of higher and lower-order questions is most effective at all ages. For older learners, increasing higher-order questions to ~50% brings significant gains.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Cymraeg — Y Gwahaniaeth Allweddol

Mae cwestiynau lefel is yn gofyn i ddisgyblion gofio. Maent yn tueddu i fod yn gaeëdig, gydag un ateb cywir, yn dechrau gyda beth, pwy, pryd, lle.

Mae cwestiynau lefel uwch yn gofyn i ddisgyblion feddwl. Maent yn agored — gydag ystod o atebion posib — yn dechrau gyda sut, pam, pa un, i ba raddau.

Cotton (1988): mae cyfuniad o gwestiynau lefel uwch ac is yn fwyaf effeithiol ar bob oedran.

🔍 Research Findings · Canfyddiadau Ymchwil

  • Wragg (1993): 57% management questions, 37% recall, only 8% higher-order in secondary lessons.
  • Cotton (1988): 60%+ of questions require only factual recall across all ages — even with older learners.
  • Kerry (1989): Only 4% of secondary questions were higher-order in observed lessons.
  • Alexander (2017): Dialogic teaching — questions should chain together; student answers are built on, not merely received.
  • Paramore (2017): Closed/convergent questions create 'cognitively restricting rituals' (Alexander, 2006) — limiting classroom dialogue.

Section 3: Wait Time & Questioning Culture Adran 3: Amser Aros a Diwylliant Cwestiynu

🇬🇧 English

Most questions are answered in less than a second — the average time teachers allow between posing a question and accepting an answer. Mary Budd Rowe's (1978) research found that increasing wait time dramatically improved the number and quality of responses.

Research also identifies 'peer fear' as the main obstacle to pupils answering: children's main fear is not of being wrong, but of looking silly — being ridiculed by the teacher or other pupils (Mitchell, Australia).

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Cymraeg

Mae'r rhan fwyaf o gwestiynau'n cael eu hateb mewn llai nag eiliad — yr amser cyfartalog y mae athrawon yn ei ganiatáu. Canfu ymchwil Mary Budd Rowe (1978) fod cynyddu'r amser aros yn gwella nifer ac ansawdd yr ymatebion yn sylweddol.

Mae ymchwil hefyd yn nodi 'ofn cymheiriaid' fel y prif rwystr i ddisgyblion ateb: nid ofn bod yn anghywir yw prif ofn plant, ond ofn edrych yn dwp — cael eu gwawdio gan yr athro neu ddisgyblion eraill.

<1 sec
Typical classroom · Dosbarth nodweddiadol

3–5 sec
Lower-order questions · Cwestiynau lefel is

Building a Questioning Culture · Adeiladu Diwylliant Cwestiynu

✅ Do this · Gwnewch hyn

  • Display key enquiry questions linked to CfW 'What Matters'
  • Celebrate wrong answers: 'Interesting — what made you think that?'
  • Allow learners to discuss before any public response
  • Model curiosity — share questions you don't yet know the answer to
  • Create a 'Question Wall' for learners to post questions throughout the topic

❌ Avoid this · Osgowch hyn

  • Answering your own question immediately after asking it
  • Hands-up only — lets the same pupils dominate every time
  • Rapid-fire questioning — fails to give accurate AfL information (Brooks & Brooks, 2001)
  • Giving weaker pupils less wait time than stronger ones
  • Allowing 'peer fear' to go unaddressed — it's the primary barrier to participation

Section 4: Questioning in the Humanities — Subject Focus Adran 4: Cwestiynu yn y Dyniaethau — Ffocws Pwnc

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Curriculum for Wales Connection · Cysylltiad Cwricwlwm i Gymru

Each Humanities discipline has its own distinctive ways of framing questions. The Curriculum for Wales emphasises enquiry, local Welsh perspectives, and the concept of cynefin (a learner's sense of place and belonging) as a foundation for wider engagement. · Mae gan bob disgyblaeth yn y Dyniaethau ei ffyrdd nodweddiadol ei hun o fframio cwestiynau. Mae Cwricwlwm i Gymru yn pwysleisio ymholi a chynefin.

📜 History · Hanes
Hanes — Ymholi Hanesyddol yng Nghwricwlwm i Gymru

In History, questions engage with the second-order concepts that define the discipline. Good historical questions connect to Welsh history and learners' own cynefin.

Cause & Consequence Change & Continuity Significance Evidence & Interpretation Similarity & Difference Historical Perspectives

Example Sequence — The Rebecca Riots · Dilyniant Enghreifftiol

  • R
    Remember "What do you already know about the Rebecca Riots?"
  • U
    Understand "Explain why toll gates caused such resentment in rural Wales."
  • An
    Analyse "What caused the unrest in rural Wales in the 1830–40s beyond the toll gates?"
  • Ev
    Evaluate "How far was the toll gate system the most significant cause of the Riots?"
  • Cr
    Create "What questions does this primary source raise about how historians have interpreted the Riots?"
🌏 Geography · Daearyddiaeth
Daearyddiaeth — Ymholi Daearyddol a Chynefin

Geography is fundamentally an enquiry-based discipline. Use the Geographical Enquiry Cycle to build question sequences. Ground enquiry in learners' cynefin before moving to the wider world.

Observe Question Investigate Analyse Conclude

Example Sequence — Flooding in South Wales · Llifogydd yn Ne Cymru

  • 1
    Observe "What do you notice about this flood map of Cardiff?"
  • 2
    Question "What questions does this raise for you about why flooding occurs here?"
  • 3
    Investigate "What evidence would help us understand the physical and human causes?"
  • 4
    Analyse "Why does flooding occur more frequently in the South Wales Valleys than the Brecon Beacons?"
  • 5
    Conclude "To what extent is global warming responsible for changes in Welsh upland ecosystems?"
💡 Cynefin tip: "What questions do you have after examining this OS map of your local cynefin?" — Starting with the familiar generates genuine curiosity before scaling up.
🕊️ Religion, Values & Ethics · Crefydd, Gwerthoedd ac Moeseg
GAE — Cwestiynu Sensitif a Diogelwch yn y Dosbarth

RVE involves some of the most profound questions humans ask. Questions must be open, exploratory and non-threatening. The CfW requires learners to explore their own and others' perspectives on religious and non-religious worldviews.

Ultimate Questions Ethical Questions Interpretive Questions

✅ Appropriate · Priodol

  • "How do different faith traditions approach the question of suffering?"
  • "What might a Muslim mean when they say 'It is God's will'?"
  • "Is it ever right to take a life? Consider different ethical frameworks."

⚠️ Avoid · Osgowch

  • "Do you believe in God?" — demands personal confession
  • "What is your religion?" — invasive and irrelevant
  • "Which religion is right about what happens after death?" — presupposes a singular answer

⚠️ Sensitivity Note · Nodyn Sensitifrwydd

Distinguish between questions about beliefs (exploring what people believe and why) and questions that demand personal confession (asking learners to state their own beliefs). Learners have the right to withhold personal beliefs. All worldviews — religious and non-religious — must be treated with equal respect and curiosity. · Rhaid gwahaniaethu rhwng cwestiynau am gredoau a chwestiynau sy'n gofyn i ddysgwyr ddatgelu eu credoau personol.

Section 5: 10 Practical Strategies for the Humanities Classroom Adran 5: 10 Strategaeth Ymarferol ar gyfer Dosbarth y Dyniaethau

1 · No Hands Up 🙅
Dim Dwylo i Fyny
Cold-call any learner using lolly sticks, a seating plan or random selector. Ensures equity — every pupil is accountable, not just volunteers.
Pair with a 'right to pass' — "I'd like to come back to that" — to reduce anxiety without removing accountability.
2 · Think–Pair–Share 👥
Meddwl–Paru–Rhannu
Pose a question; learners discuss in pairs before sharing with the class. Reduces 'peer fear' by allowing rehearsal before public performance.
"Should we eat meat? Discuss with your partner first." — works well for ethical dilemmas in RVE and contested historical judgements.
3 · Pose, Pause, Pounce, Bounce 🏀
Gofyn, Oedi, Cyfeirio, Bownsio
Ask the class, wait 5–10 seconds, direct to one learner, then bounce to a second for comment or challenge.
"What does this source tell us about attitudes to Welsh identity? [10 sec] Sion, what do you think? Nia — do you agree?"
4 · Hot Seat 🎭
Y Sedd Boeth
A learner answers in character as a historical figure, geographer or religious leader. The class questions them.
History: a Rebecca Riots leader. Geography: a coastal resident facing managed retreat. RVE: a key religious figure on suffering.
5 · Question Wall 📌
Wal Gwestiynau
A display where learners pin their questions throughout the topic. Prioritise wonder, not just recall. Return at the end — which have been answered?
"Begin this unit on Climate Change with 'What questions do you have?' Review the wall at the end — what remains unanswered?"
6 · Here's the Answer… 🔀
Dyma'r Ateb…
"The answer is 1847. What might the question be?" — multiple valid questions are possible. Promotes out-of-the-box thinking.
"The answer is 47%. What was the question?" — sparks debate about what data means in a Geography lesson on urbanisation.
7 · Snowball ❄️
Pêl Eira
Write a question, pass to partner who improves it, pass on again. Questions grow in complexity. Scaffolds those who struggle to generate questions independently.
Learner A writes a Level 1 question; Learner B rewrites at Level 3; Learner C pushes to Level 5. Final question is often genuinely challenging.
8 · Odd One Out 🔍
Yr Un Allan o'r Cyffredin
"Which is the odd one out? Justify your choice." All answers justified by reasoning are valid — the justification is the learning.
RVE: "Mosque, church, synagogue — odd one out?" History: "Chartists, Suffragettes, Rebecca Rioters — odd one out? Justify."
9 · Eavesdropping 👂
Clustfeinio
Circulate during group work and pose targeted questions to each group based on what you hear. Allows differentiated questioning in real time.
"I heard you say it was unfair — can you explain what standard of fairness you're applying? Who decides?"
10 · Exit Question 🚪
Cwestiwn Ymadael
A higher-order question posed at the end of the lesson that learners take home. Answered at the start of the next lesson.
"Was the Industrial Revolution a good thing for Wales? Come with evidence next lesson." — directly links to CfW enquiry cycle.
