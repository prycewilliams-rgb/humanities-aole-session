Section 1: The Curriculum for Wales Framework Adran 1: Fframwaith Cwricwlwm i Gymru

🇬🇧 English

This session explores one of the most important — and sometimes underused — concepts in the Humanities AoLE: historical interpretations. As a PGCE trainee in Wales, you must understand the CfW's distinct requirements, not the English National Curriculum. Welsh history is mandatory, and interpretations are a named concept within the framework.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Cymraeg

Mae'r sesiwn hon yn archwilio un o'r cysyniadau pwysicaf — ac weithiau'r mwyaf tan-ddefnyddiog — yn MDP y Dyniaethau: dehongliadau hanesyddol. Fel hyfforddai TGCH yng Nghymru, rhaid i chi ddeall gofynion penodol y CfW — nid Cwricwlwm Cenedlaethol Lloegr. Mae hanes Cymru yn orfodol, ac mae dehongliadau yn gysyniad a enwir yn y fframwaith.

The Four Purposes — How Interpretations Fit · Y Pedwar Diben

🎓 Ambitious, Capable Learners · Dysgwyr Uchelgeisiol
  • Apply disciplinary thinking to historical interpretations
  • Evaluate why accounts of Wales differ
  • Frame and investigate their own enquiry questions
💡 Enterprising, Creative Contributors · Cyfranwyr Mentrus
  • Produce their own historical representations
  • Use visual, digital and creative media to interpret the past
  • Communicate understanding in diverse, non-written forms
🌍 Ethical, Informed Citizens · Dinasyddion Gwybodus
  • Understand how Welsh history has been told — and sometimes silenced
  • Appreciate diverse perspectives: BAME histories, Welsh-language communities
  • Recognise that interpretations can empower or marginalise communities
❤️ Healthy, Confident Individuals · Unigolion Hyderus
  • Develop identity and cynefin through local and national Welsh history
  • See their own locality reflected in historical accounts
  • Understand their place in the story of Wales

Five Statements of What Matters — Humanities AoLE · Y Pum Datganiad

1

Enquiry, Evidence & Disciplinary Thinking / Ymholi, Tystiolaeth a Meddwl Disgyblaethol

Learners are curious, question critically and use evidence to build supported answers about Welsh history and the wider world.

2

⭐ Interpreting Human Experience / Dehongli Profiad Dynol — the key statement for this session

Learners explore how they and others view, interpret and represent human experiences. This includes: representations, perspectives, interpretations, significance and validity — all named concepts within the AoLE. This is not optional extra — it is core.

3

Identity, Heritage & Cynefin / Hunaniaeth, Treftadaeth a Chynefin

An appreciation of Welsh identity, heritage and cynefin — the history of Wales and the world — helps learners build their sense of self and of belonging.

4

Diversity & Human Society / Amrywiaeth a Chymdeithas Ddynol

Consistent exposure to the story of Wales and the wider world enables understanding of complex, pluralistic, diverse societies — including BAME communities in Wales.

5

Citizenship & Action / Dinasyddiaeth a Chamau Gweithredu

Learners take considered, ethical action as responsible citizens of Wales and the world.

"Cynefin — the place where we feel we belong, where the people and landscape around us are familiar, and the sights and sounds are reassuringly recognisable. Learners should be grounded in an understanding of the identities, landscapes and histories that come together to form their cynefin."

— Curriculum for Wales, Humanities Area of Learning and Experience (2022)

Section 2: Sources vs Interpretations — A Crucial Distinction Adran 2: Ffynonellau v Dehongliadau — Gwahaniaeth Allweddol

🇬🇧 English

One of the most common confusions in history teaching is treating interpretations and sources as the same thing. They are not. Getting this distinction right is fundamental to teaching the CfW's Statement 2 effectively.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Cymraeg

Un o'r dryswch mwyaf cyffredin mewn addysgu hanes yw trin dehongliadau a ffynonellau fel yr un peth. Nid ydynt yr un peth. Mae cael y gwahaniaeth hwn yn gywir yn sylfaenol i addysgu Datganiad 2 y CfW yn effeithiol.

Section 3: The Three Articles — Reading Guides Adran 3: Y Tair Erthygl — Canllawiau Darllen

📋 The Three Articles · Y Tair Erthygl

All three articles are from Teaching History 130 (March 2008) — reproduced for educational purposes and available as PDFs alongside this session. Read at least one in full before completing the reading frames below. · Mae'r tair erthygl o Teaching History 130 — sydd ar gael fel PDFau ochr yn ochr â'r sesiwn hon.

1

Redrawing the Renaissance: non-verbal assessment in Year 7

Matt Stanford — Teaching History 130, pp.4–11

Stanford argues that pupils with a flair for the visual were unfairly excluded from historical assessment. He designed a non-verbal task for Year 7 on the Renaissance — pupils create an image representing the period, assessed through what they selected to include and how they represented it.

📄 PDF provided — read pp.4–11
2

Thinking across time: planning and teaching the story of power and democracy at KS3

Ian Dawson — Teaching History 130, pp.14–23

Dawson argues that pupils leave school unable to tell coherent historical stories across time. His solution: 'thematic stories' — narratives that follow one theme (e.g. power and democracy) from past to present. He shows how such stories are themselves constructs — interpretations — that could be told differently.

📄 PDF provided — read pp.14–23
3

Stepping into the past: using images to travel through time

Caille Sugarman-Banaszak — Teaching History 130, pp.24–29

Sugarman-Banaszak uses images — including her own family photographs — as 'ladders' to the past. She explores how photographs, artwork and 'then and now' comparisons help pupils both receive and produce historical interpretations, including through a virtual museum of the Shoah.

📄 PDF provided — read pp.24–29

Q3 How could Stanford's approach be adapted for a Welsh historical topic? (e.g. Changing Wales, a local colliery community, Welsh-language history) Give a specific example.
Q4 — CfW Discussion Stanford argues a drawn image can be a valid historical interpretation. How does this connect to Statement 2 (interpreting human experience) and the CfW's creative contributor purpose? What assessment criteria would you use?

📄 Article 2: Dawson (2008) — Thinking Across Time

Q1 What is a 'thematic story', and why does Dawson prefer this term to 'framework' or 'theme'? What does this linguistic choice tell us about his view of historical knowledge?
Q2 Why is a thematic story itself an interpretation? What does Dawson mean when he says it 'could be told differently, depending on viewpoint or choice of content'?
Q3 — Welsh Thematic Story Sketch a Welsh thematic story relevant to your school placement (e.g. the story of the Welsh language, coal and Wales, democracy in Wales, women's lives in Wales). What key turning points would you include — and what would you leave out?
Q4 — CfW Discussion The CfW requires 'consistent exposure to the story of Wales'. Dawson's approach suggests this story is a construct. How can you teach learners that the 'story of Wales' is shaped by selection — without undermining their sense of cynefin and identity?

Section 4: Three Classroom Strategies — Welsh Adaptations Adran 4: Tair Strategaeth Dosbarth — Addasiadau Cymreig

🎨
Strategy 1: Non-Verbal Assessment (Stanford) Strategaeth 1: Asesiad Di-eiriau — Addysgu Ymdeimlad o Gyfnod

The core insight: Stanford realised that pupils with a flair for the visual were being unfairly excluded from historical success because history assessments almost exclusively rewarded the written word. He designed a task where Year 7 pupils drew an image representing the Renaissance — assessed not on artistic quality but on what historical understanding their choices revealed.

The mark scheme (Figure 4) assessed four dimensions: Things from the Renaissance, Ideas from the Renaissance, Would it have pleased Lorenzo the Magnificent?, and Artistic techniques from the Renaissance. Each dimension had descriptors from 'none included' to 'I can see lots.' This is a historical mark scheme, not an art mark scheme.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Welsh Adaptation · Addasiad Cymreig Ask pupils to draw the 'most Welsh' image for Changing Wales 1870–1914 — or design a book cover selecting four images to represent population change, heavy industry, leisure and the Welsh language. The Changing Wales coat of arms task is essentially Stanford's approach applied to Welsh history. Assess: What has the pupil included? What does their selection tell us about their understanding of significance? Which CfW Statement does this address? (Statement 2 and Statement 3: cynefin/identity)

🔗 CfW Connection · Cysylltiad CfW

The CfW's 'enterprising, creative contributor' purpose explicitly legitimises visual and non-verbal expression. The Expressive Arts AoLE links also support cross-curricular work. Non-verbal assessments of historical understanding are not a soft option — they can be more rigorous than essays when properly designed. Statement 2 is clear: learners produce their own representations.

📖
Strategy 2: Thematic Stories (Dawson) Strategaeth 2: Straeon Thematig — Cynllunio Ar Draws Amser

The core insight: Dawson argues that pupils emerge from school with patchy knowledge of individual events but no coherent big picture — no story they can tell. His solution is 'thematic stories': narratives that follow one theme across time, tell-able in a single lesson, and expandable into depth across years of schooling. Critically, a thematic story is always a construct — it could be told differently.

Key planning principles: Plan backwards — what will learners take away? Make the story tell-able in one lesson before expanding. Use ICT (Movie Maker, PowerPoint) to create stories learners can return to. Ask: if you're never going to refer back to this topic, why are you teaching it?

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Welsh Thematic Stories · Straeon Thematig Cymreig The Welsh language — from the 'Welsh Not' to the Welsh Language Act and S4C. Coal and iron in Wales — growth, community, disaster, deindustrialisation. Women's lives in Wales — from the pit brow to the Senedd. Democracy in Wales — Chartism, suffrage, devolution. Wales and the world — Welsh communities in Patagonia, the USA, migration in and out. Each story is an interpretation: whose voices are foregrounded? What's left out? How does BAME Welsh history (Tiger Bay, Butetown) fit?

🔗 CfW Connection · Cysylltiad CfW

The CfW requires 'consistent exposure to the story of their locality and the story of Wales.' Dawson's approach shows this story is a construct. Teaching learners to see it as such is exactly what Statement 2 demands: exploring how we represent and interpret human experiences. The local/national Welsh dimension threads through all thematic stories.

🖼️
Strategy 3: Images as Interpretations (Sugarman-Banaszak) Strategaeth 3: Delweddau fel Dehongliadau — Camu i'r Gorffennol

The core insight: Images are more than visual sources — they are interpretations. Sugarman-Banaszak uses her own family photographs as classroom 'ladders' to the past, grounding historical enquiry in personal connection. She also uses 'then and now' comparisons, the Bosch 'Where's Wally?' technique, and asks pupils to design a virtual museum that memorialises the Shoah.

The virtual museum task: Pupils compile visual and written material, explaining in a final statement 'the purpose of the images and story within.' Both the art and images they create, and those they curate, allow them to step back into the past whilst remaining grounded in the present. The museum is itself a historical interpretation.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Welsh Adaptations · Addasiadau Cymreig Then & now: a Welsh colliery village before/after pit closure — the absence in the 'now' photograph IS the interpretation. Tiger Bay / Cardiff Bay: once a diverse maritime community, largely demolished in the 1970s — what does the Senedd building's presence/absence tell us? Welsh-language signage before/after the Language Act. Learners design a 'virtual museum of Wales' — curation is interpretation. Use learners' own cynefin photography: what does their locality look like, and what does it remember?

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Cynefin Connection · Cysylltiad Cynefin

Sugarman-Banaszak's personal family photographs create a three-way bond between teacher, pupil and history. In Wales, learners' own cynefin — their locality, community, family stories — can serve the same function. A photograph of a learner's grandparent's mining village, or a bilingual street sign, is a starting point for asking: whose story of this place has been told? And whose has been left out?

Key Concepts Glossary · Geirfa Cysyniadau Allweddol

Historical interpretation
A representation of the past produced after the event, shaped by purpose, audience, selection of evidence and cultural context. In Wales, all interpretations of Welsh history are also shaped by political, linguistic and community context.
Cynefin
The Welsh concept of a sense of place and belonging — the landscape, people, language and histories that feel familiar and home. Central to the CfW Humanities AoLE; interpretations of local Welsh history engage learners' cynefin directly.
Statement 2 (CfW)
The Humanities AoLE statement that requires learners to explore representations, perspectives, interpretations, significance and validity — named concepts, not optional extras. The core statement for this session.
Thematic story (Dawson)
A narrative that follows one theme across time — e.g. the story of the Welsh language, or democracy in Wales. A construct that could be told differently, depending on viewpoint or selection. Must be tell-able in one lesson.
Non-verbal assessment (Stanford)
Assessing historical understanding through a medium other than writing — a drawn image, a shield, a book cover. Assessed on what the pupil selected to include, not on artistic quality. Particularly relevant to the CfW's creative contributor purpose.
Welsh dimension
The mandatory requirement in the CfW that Welsh history, culture, language and society are woven through all learning in the Humanities AoLE. Not a bolt-on — it is the core context for all interpretations work.
Progression steps (CfW)
CfW descriptors of what learners can do at different stages (ages 5, 8, 11, 14, 16). In Humanities, progression in interpretations moves from identifying that accounts differ to critically evaluating why — and to producing increasingly sophisticated representations.
