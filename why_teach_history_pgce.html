Section 2: Wineburg — Unnatural and Essential Adran 2: Wineburg — Annaturiol ac Hanfodol

"Historical thinking goes against the grain of how we ordinarily think."

— Sam Wineburg, Teaching History 129 (2007)

🇬🇧 English — The Central Argument

Wineburg argues that historical thinking is both 'unnatural' and 'essential'. It is unnatural because it runs counter to our everyday cognitive habits — snap judgements, vivid associations, surface-level pattern matching. It is essential because without it we cannot navigate the complexities of living in time.

He demonstrates this through an experiment comparing how a 17-year-old student, primary teachers, and doctoral historians read the same historical document — a Harrison Proclamation from 1892.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 Cymraeg — Y Ddadl Ganolog

Mae Wineburg yn dadlau bod meddwl hanesyddol yn 'annaturiol' ac yn 'hanfodol'. Mae'n annaturiol oherwydd ei fod yn mynd yn groes i'n harferion gwybyddol bob dydd — barnau brysiog, cysylltiadau bywiog, cyfatebiaeth arwynebol. Mae'n hanfodol oherwydd hebddo ni allwn lywio cymhlethdodau byw mewn amser.

Mae'n dangos hyn drwy arbrawf yn cymharu sut roedd disgybl 17 oed, athrawon cynradd, a myfyrwyr doethuriaeth yn darllen yr un ddogfen hanesyddol.

Two Key Cognitive Concepts · Dau Gysyniad Gwybyddol Allweddol

Spread of Activation · Lledaenu Actifadu

Semantic information is organised in associative memory networks. Once a 'node' is activated, related nodes are more likely to be activated. E.g. flashing 'car' makes you quicker to think of BMW than bread. · Mae gwybodaeth eirfaol wedi'i threfnu mewn rhwydweithiau cof cysylladol.

Availability Heuristic · Hiwristig Argaeledd

Thinkers privilege information that is vivid and easily available in memory over more pallid but often more probative information. E.g. citing a grandfather who smoked but lived to 95 over statistical mortality data. · Mae meddylwyr yn blaenoriaethu gwybodaeth fywiog.

The Harrison Proclamation Experiment · Arbrawf Cyhoeddiad Harrison

❌ Jacob — 17-year-old student · Disgybl 17 oed

"He gazed cursorily at the sheet and then launched into a mini-speech about Columbus, intoning with youthful exuberance his zeitgeist's politically correct shibboleths. He was 'critical' of Columbus — but 'being critical' and 'thinking critically' are not the same."

The Columbus node activated → availability heuristic dominated → context ignored · Nod Columbus wedi'i actifadu → cyd-destun wedi'i anwybyddu

✅ Matt — PhD History student · Myfyriwr Hanes PhD

"Okay it's 1892, so it's the 400th anniversary. Benjamin Harrison… The 1890s, the beginning of the Progressive Era, end of the century, closing of the frontier, Frederick Jackson Turner… Biggest wave of immigration in US history. That's it."

Puzzled about 1892, not 1492 — asked what the document meant in its moment of production · Cwestiynodd 1892, nid 1492

🔑 The Key Insight — 'Specification of Ignorance' · 'Penodi Anwybodaeth'

Historians use a document not merely to issue a judgement about it, but to stimulate new questions and identify gaps in knowledge. Students typically encountered the document and issued judgements. Historians positioned themselves to learn. This is why we call history a discipline — it cultivates modes of thought that counterbalance haste and avert premature judgement. · Safonau meddwl sy'n gwrthbwyso brys ac yn atal barn brydlon.

Secondary History: Current Themes

Terry Haydn — in Davies (ed.) Debates in History Teaching (2011)

Haydn surveys secondary history teaching in England and Wales — challenges teachers face, the contested nature of the subject, and the professional context you are entering. Available via university library: search 'Debates in History Teaching Davies'.

Q1 What does Haydn identify as the main purposes of history teaching? Are they in tension?
Q2 What does he say about the relationship between subject knowledge and pedagogical knowledge?
Q3 What challenges are specific to history (rather than generic teaching challenges)?
Q4 What would Haydn say to a headteacher proposing to replace history with a generic 'humanities' course?
2

Understanding History Teaching — Chapter 1

Husbands, Kitson & Pendry (2003) — OUP. pp. 1–22

The opening chapter argues that history is one of the most ideologically contested school subjects, drawing on research with teachers and pupils to show how history teachers think about their subject. Available via university library: search 'Understanding History Teaching Husbands'.

Q1 Why do the authors describe history as 'ideologically contested'? Do you agree?
Q2 What distinction is drawn between 'content knowledge' and 'pedagogical content knowledge' in history?
Q3 How do the teachers in their research justify teaching history? Which justification is most convincing?
Q4 What does the chapter suggest about the relationship between a teacher's understanding of history and how they teach it?
3

Understanding History Teaching — Chapter 8

Husbands, Kitson & Pendry (2003) — OUP. pp. 141–155

The closing chapter asks what it means to be a history teacher in the contemporary school — arguing that being a subject specialist and a professional educator are not in tension but mutually reinforcing. Available via university library.

Q1 What should be the overarching aim of school history? How does this compare with your expectations?
Q2 What kind of relationship with the subject does being a good history teacher require?
Q3 How do the authors address the tension between 'covering content' and developing historical thinking skills?
Q4 What practical implications does the argument have for how you might plan and teach a history lesson?

Unnatural and Essential: The Nature of Historical Thinking

Sam Wineburg — Teaching History 129 (2007), pp. 6–11

Full text included in your reading booklet (Article A). Wineburg demonstrates why historical thinking is 'unnatural' through a striking experiment comparing how students, teachers and historians read the same document. Also available via the Historical Association.

Q1 What does Wineburg mean by 'unnatural'? Why did this claim cause controversy?

Key Debates to Discuss · Dadleuon Allweddol i'w Trafod

Q1

What's the point in studying the past? / Beth yw'r diben astudio'r gorffennol?

Is history studied for its own sake, for citizenship, for transferable skills, for national identity — or all of these? Who decides? · A yw hanes yn cael ei astudio er ei fwyn ei hun, ar gyfer dinasyddiaeth, sgiliau trosglwyddadwy, hunaniaeth genedlaethol — neu'r cwbl?

Q2

Who should decide what is taught? / Pwy ddylai benderfynu beth sy'n cael ei addysgu?

Government? Exam boards? Teachers? Pupils? Communities? What happens when these conflict — as they do in Wales around Welsh history, colonialism, and whose voices are centred? · Llywodraeth? Byrddau arholi? Athrawon? Disgyblion? Cymunedau?

Q3

Knowledge or skills — which is more important? / Gwybodaeth neu sgiliau — pa un sy'n bwysicach?

Can you develop historical thinking skills without substantive content knowledge? Or does skills-led history risk becoming hollow? Where does this debate sit in the Welsh context? · A ellir datblygu sgiliau meddwl hanesyddol heb gynnwys gwybodaeth sylweddol?

Q4

Should GCSE History remain single tier in Wales? / A ddylai TGAU Hanes aros yn un haen yng Nghymru?

WJEC offers a single-tier entry for GCSE History. What are the pedagogical and equity arguments for and against? How does this affect your teaching? · Mae CBAC yn cynnig mynediad un haen ar gyfer TGAU Hanes. Beth yw'r dadleuon addysgegol a thegwch?

Q5

Only WJEC in Wales at GCSE and A Level? / Dim ond CBAC yng Nghymru?

Welsh schools largely take WJEC specifications — what are the strengths and limitations of this? Does it serve the diversity of learners in Wales? · Mae ysgolion Cymru yn bennaf yn cymryd manylebau CBAC — beth yw'r cryfderau a'r cyfyngiadau?

Q6

Did the history you studied represent you? / A oedd yr hanes a astudioch yn eich cynrychioli chi?

Whose history was taught in your school? Whose was absent? What does this tell us about power, curriculum design, and whose stories are considered historically significant in Wales? · Hanes pwy gafodd ei addysgu yn eich ysgol chi?
